CHAPTER XIII — Critical Commentary & Notes¶

On Its Fourth Ineffable Effect

Historical and Philological Commentary¶

  1. Campanus XIII.4 vs. Heiberg :cite:`Euclid XIII.5 <euclid:elements:XIII.5>` (Ordering Inversion in Book XIII): In Campanus of Novara’s Latin edition of Elements Book XIII, Heiberg’s Propositions 4 and 5 are transposed! Specifically:

    • Campanus XIII.4 (= Heiberg Euclid XIII.5): If a straight line $AB$ is cut in extreme and mean ratio at $C$ (with greater segment $AC$), and $AD = AC$ is added to $AB$, then the whole $BD = AB + AC$ is cut in extreme and mean ratio at $A$, with greater segment $AB$.

    • Campanus XIII.5 (= Heiberg Euclid XIII.4): If a straight line $AB$ is cut in extreme and mean ratio, the sum of the squares on the whole $AB$ and the lesser segment $BC$ is triple the square on the greater segment $AC$.

    Pacioli follows Campanus’s numbering strictly in Chapters XIII and XIV: Chapter XIII gives Heiberg Euclid XIII.5 (citing la quarta del terzodecimo), and Chapter XIV gives Heiberg Euclid XIII.4 (citing la quinta del 13.).

  2. Difference of Squares Identity: Notice how cleanly the product of the conjugate binomial and apotome evaluates: $(\sqrt{125} - 5)(\sqrt{125} + 5) = 125 - 25 = 100 = 10^2$.

Constantin Winterberg’s Commentary (1889 Edition)¶

Note 4 (Cap. XIII): Algebraic Proof of the Fourth Effect (Euclid XIII.5) (Sachliche Anmerkungen #4, Cap. XIII, p. 203, l. 7 from bottom)

In algebraic form, let the segment $bc$ be divided in extreme and mean ratio at $d$ ($cd > bd$), and let $ac = cd$ be adjoined to form the whole line $ad = ac + bc$:

  • Hypothesis: $cd^2 = bc \cdot bd$ and $ac = cd$.

  • Claim: $bc^2 = ad \cdot cd = ad \cdot ac$.

  • Proof: By hypothesis, $cd^2 = bc(bc - cd) = bc^2 - bc \cdot cd$, whence

    \[bc^2 = cd^2 + bc \cdot cd = cd(cd + bc) = cd(ac + bc) = ad \cdot cd. \quad \text{Q.E.D.}\]

Anmerkung 4 (Cap. XIII, S. 203): Mathematischer Nachweis des vierten Effects (Euklid XIII.5)

In mathematischer Form (wenn $bc$ in $d$ stetig getheilt und $ac = cd$ angesetzt wird):

  • Voraussetzung: $cd^2 = bc \cdot bd$ und $ac = cd$.

  • Behauptung: $bc^2 = ad \cdot cd$.

  • Beweis: Nach Voraussetzung ist $cd^2 = bc(bc - cd)$, folglich:

    \[ad \cdot cd = (ac + bc)\,cd = cd^2 + bc \cdot cd = bc^2. \quad \text{w. z. b. w.}\]
