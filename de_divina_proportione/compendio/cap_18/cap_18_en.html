Chapter XVIII: On Its Ninth Effect, Surpassing All the Others¶

On Its Ninth Effect, Surpassing All the Others

If an equilateral pentagon be formed in a circle, and opposite two of its adjacent angles two straight lines [diagonals] be subtended from the endpoints of their sides, those two lines will of necessity divide one another according to our proportion, and each of their greater parts will always be equal to the side of the said pentagon ([XIII.8]).

Geometric and Numerical Demonstration

For example, let the equilateral pentagon be abcde, and from the endpoints c and a let the chord ac be drawn subtending the angle b; and from the endpoints b and e let the other chord be be drawn subtending the angle a.

I say that these two lines ac and be divide one another at the point f according to the proportion having a mean and two extremes ([XIII.8]), and the greater part of each is exactly equal to the side of the said pentagon:

  • Of the line ac, the greater part is cf, and the lesser part is af;

  • Of the line be, the greater part is ef, and the lesser part is bf;

  • And each of these greater parts (cf and ef) is always equal to the side of the said pentagon (ab $= $ bc $= $ cd $= $ de $= $ ea).

By mathematicians these two lines (ac and be) are called by another name the chords of the pentagonal angle (corde del’angolo pentagonico).

Thus, if each of the said chords (ac and be) were $10$ (for they are equal when their pentagon in the circle is equilateral and equiangular):

  • cf would be $\sqrt{125} - 5$, and af would be $15 - \sqrt{125}$;

  • The part ef would similarly be $\sqrt{125} - 5$, and bf would be $15 - \sqrt{125}$;

  • And the side of the pentagon itself would likewise be $\sqrt{125} - 5$!

All of this the eighth [and eleventh] proposition of the Thirteenth Book ([XIII.8], [XIII.11]) demonstrates geometrically in a beautiful manner. And through this effect, from knowledge of the side of the pentagon we can arrive at knowledge of all its diagonals (corde) and all their parts; and conversely, from knowledge of the diagonals we can arrive at knowledge of the side and of the segments of the said diagonals—operating arithmetically and geometrically just as we have taught in our aforesaid work [Summa] how to handle them with all diligence by means of binomials (binomii) and other irrational lines, whereof our Philosopher treats in his Tenth Book, and which he demonstrates for a line in the eleventh of the Second ([II.11]) and the twenty-ninth [thirtieth] of the Sixth ([VI.30]). Thus one easily arrives at the knowledge of the one from the other in every way, which is a thing of the greatest utility in our scientific and speculative inquiries.
