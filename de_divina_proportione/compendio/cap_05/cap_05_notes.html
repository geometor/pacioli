CHAPTER V — Critical Commentary & Notes¶

On the Fitting Title of the Present Treatise

Historical and Philological Commentary¶

  1. Coining the Name *:term:`Divina Proportione <divina-proportione>`*: Prior to Pacioli’s treatise, Euclid’s division of a line in extreme and mean ratio (Euclid VI.def.3, $\ddot{\alpha}\kappa\rho\omicron\varsigma \kappa\alpha\grave{\iota} \mu\acute{\epsilon}\sigma\omicron\varsigma \lambda\acute{\omicron}\gamma\omicron\varsigma$) was known in medieval Latin exclusively by Gerard of Cremona’s and Campanus of Novara’s rendering proportio habens medium et duo extrema (“proportion having a mean and two extremes”). Chapter V is the historic locus where Pacioli christens the ratio $\phi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$ the :term:`*Divine Proportion <divina-proportione>`* (:term:`Divina Proportione <divina-proportione>`), grounding the name in five Scholastic-Platonic analogies:

    1. Unicity: Unlike multiplex or superparticular ratios ($2:1, 3:1, 3:2, 4:3, \dots$), which form infinite families of species, extreme and mean ratio is unique (una sola e non più).

    2. Trinitarian Structure: Continuous proportion requires a minimum of three terms ($a+b : a :: a : b$), mirroring the una substantia in tribus personis of Trinitarian theology.

    3. Apophatic Ineffability: The incommensurability of $\phi$ (Euclid XIII.6) is compared to negative (apophatic) theology: just as God transcends finite verbal definition, $\phi$ transcends rational numerical expression (occulta e secreta).

    4. Self-Similarity and Invariance: Echoing the Neoplatonic and Augustinian maxim that God is totus in toto et totus in qualibet parte, $\phi$ remains invariant across all scales and self-replicates upon subdivision or addition (Euclid XIII.4, Euclid XIV.2).

    5. Cosmological Formative Principle (Plato’s *:cite:`Timaeus <plato:timaeus:53c-56c>`*): Combining Aristotle’s :cite:`De Caelo <aristotle:de-caelo:III.8>` (the celestial :term:`quinta essentia <quinta-essentia>`) with Plato’s :cite:`Timaeus <plato:timaeus:53c-56c>` 53c–56c (Tetrahedron = Fire, Cube = Earth, Octahedron = Air, Icosahedron = Water, Dodecahedron = the Cosmos/Heaven), Pacioli argues that because the regular pentagon and dodecahedron—and the mutual proportions of all five Platonic solids inscribed in a single sphere (Euclid XIII.13–Euclid XIII.18)—depend essentially on $\phi$, the Divine Proportion is the formal geometric principle of cosmic order.
