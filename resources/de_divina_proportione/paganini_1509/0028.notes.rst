Cap. XVI — On Its Seventh Inestimable Effect
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Historical and Philological Commentary
--------------------------------------

1. **Hexagon and Decagon Inscribed in the Same Circle (Euclid XIII.9)**: By :cite:`Euclid IV.15 <euclid:elements:IV.15>`.p, the side $s_6$ of the regular hexagon inscribed in a circle of radius $R$ is equal to the radius itself ($s_6 = R$, so the diameter is $2R = 2(\sqrt{125}-5) = \sqrt{500}-10$). Proposition 9 of Book XIII proves that when $s_6$ and the side $s_{10}$ of the regular decagon inscribed in the same circle are placed in a straight line, the whole line $s_6 + s_{10}$ is cut in :term:`extreme and mean ratio <extrema-et-media-ratio>`, and $s_6$ is the greater segment:
   $$(s_6 + s_{10}) : s_6 = s_6 : s_{10} = \phi$$

Cap. XVII — On the Eighth Effect, the Converse of the Preceding
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Historical and Philological Commentary
--------------------------------------

1. **Ptolemy's *:cite:`Almagest <ptolemy:almagest:I.9>`* Book I, Ch. 9/10 (Calculation of the Table of Chords)**: Pacioli cites Gerard of Cremona's Latin translation of Ptolemy's *:cite:`Almagest <ptolemy:almagest:I.9>`* (*Dictio prima*, cap. 9 in manuscript/printed divisions corresponding to Heiberg Book I, Ch. 10, *De magnitudine rectarum linearum quae in circulo ducuntur*). To construct his trigonometric table of chords, :person:`Ptolemy <ptolemy>` divides the diameter into $120$ parts (so the radius / hexagon chord $\text{crd}(60^\circ) = 60$), and uses :cite:`Euclid XIII.9 <euclid:elements:XIII.9>` and :cite:`Euclid XIII.10 <euclid:elements:XIII.10>` simultaneously to compute the chord of $36^\circ$ (the side of the regular decagon, $\text{crd}(36^\circ) = \sqrt{5 \cdot 30^2} - 30 = \sqrt{4500} - 30 \approx 37;4,55$) and the chord of $72^\circ$ (the side of the regular pentagon).

Cap. XVIII — On Its Ninth Effect, Surpassing All the Others
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Historical and Philological Commentary
--------------------------------------

1. **Intersecting Diagonals of the Regular Pentagon (Euclid XIII.8 and Euclid XIII.11)**: Proposition 8 of Book XIII proves that if two diagonals :g:`ac` and :g:`be` of an equilateral and equiangular pentagon :g:`abcde` intersect at :g:`f`, both diagonals cut one another in :term:`extreme and mean ratio <extrema-et-media-ratio>` ($ac : cf = cf : af$ and $be : ef = ef : bf$), and their greater segments equal the pentagon's side ($cf = ef = ab$). :person:`Pacioli <pacioli>` also cites :cite:`Euclid XIII.11 <euclid:elements:XIII.11>` (which proves that the side of the regular pentagon inscribed in a circle with rational diameter is the irrational line called a *minor*, $\dot{\varepsilon}\lambda\acute{\alpha}\sigma\sigma\omega\nu$, constructed from a fourth binomial and fourth :term:`apotome <residuo>`) because his *Libellus* (Tractatus I) and *Summa* extensively use :cite:`Euclid XIII.8 <euclid:elements:XIII.8>` and :cite:`Euclid XIII.11 <euclid:elements:XIII.11>` together to calculate all dimensions of the pentagon and :term:`dodecahedron <dodecahedron>`.
