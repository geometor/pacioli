CHAPTER XLV — Critical Commentary & Notes

Geometric Insight of Campanus XV.11: In Euclid’s construction of the dodecahedron (Euclid XIII.17), 6 of the 30 edges of the dodecahedron are the roof ridges \(vx\) centered over the 6 faces of the inscribed cube. The midpoints of those 6 edges lie on the 3 coordinate axes of the cube at distance \(\frac{1}{2}s_{\text{cube}} + \text{lesser} = \frac{\varphi}{2}s_{\text{cube}} = \frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}a_{12}\) from the origin. Because the 3 coordinate axes are orthogonal and equal in length, those 6 edge-midpoints are the exact vertices of a regular octahedron of edge \(a_{8} = \frac{\varphi^{2}}{\sqrt{2}}a_{12}\)!

Intersecting Diagonals of the Regular Pentagon (Euclid XIII.8 and Euclid XIII.11): Proposition 8 of Book XIII proves that if two diagonals ac and be of an equilateral and equiangular pentagon abcde intersect at f, both diagonals cut one another in extreme and mean ratio (\(ac : cf = cf : af\) and \(be : ef = ef : bf\)), and their greater segments equal the pentagon’s side (\(cf = ef = ab\)). Direct Calculation of the Decagon from the Radius (\(R = 10\)): Whereas Chapters XVI and XVII took \(s_6 + s_{10} = 10\) (so that the radius \(R = s_6 = \sqrt{125} - 5\)), Chapter XX takes the hexagon side (the circle’s radius \(R\)) itself as \(10\) (so the circle’s diameter is \(20\)). Johannes Kepler’s *:term:`Stella Octangula <octahedron-elevatum>`* and the Volume Ratio: Slicing away the four right-angled corner pyramids (\(abcf\), \(acdh\), \(aefh\), \(cfgh\)) from the cube of side \(s\) leaves the regular tetrahedron \(afch\) of edge \(s\sqrt{2}\). HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES

Modern readers sometimes treat Particularis de computis et scripturis (fols. 198v–210v) as an isolated commercial manual accidentally bound inside a mathematical encyclopedia. Viewed within Pacioli’s architectural plan, however, the Venetian Quaderno is the algebraic mirror of Euclidean balance: just as Cossic restauratio (al-jabr, adding equal terms to both sides of an equation under [I.cn.2]) preserves equality between unknown and known magnitudes, posting every commercial event simultaneously to left-hand Dare and right-hand Avere preserves the global conservation law \(\sum \text{Dare} = \sum \text{Avere}\) across hundreds of heterogeneous accounts. Although the Ragusan diplomat Benedetto Cotrugli had drafted a brief manuscript chapter on double-entry in 1458 (Libro de l’arte de la mercatura, unprinted until 1573), Pacioli’s 1494 Summa was the first printed codification, instantaneously translated and adapted across Antwerp (Jan Ympyn, 1543), Nuremberg (Wolfgang Schweicker, 1549), London (Hugh Oldcastle, 1543), and Amsterdam (Simon Stevin, 1608).

HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES

Before November 1494, European mathematical print culture was sharply bifurcated: Erhard Ratdolt’s 1482 Venice Elementa served Latin-reading university scholars, whereas short commercial arte minore pamphlets (such as the 1478 Treviso Arithmetic and Pietro Borghi’s 1484 Opera de Arithmetica) gave merchants algorithmic recipes stripped of Euclidean proof. Pacioli’s deliberate choice to compose a 600-page folio in Northern-Tuscan/Venetian volgare while retaining Latin proposition headings, marginal Euclidean citations, and Boethian terminology united the scuola d’abbaco, the artist-architect’s bottega, and the university chair of mathematics into a single readership. Notice also Pacioli’s inclusion of Perspective alongside Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, and Astronomy as a fifth mathematical discipline—anticipating his fuller argument in *De Divina Proportione*, Compendio Cap. III in honor of Piero della Francesca.

HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES

From Heron's Metrica (I.8) through Al-Khwārizmī, Leonardo Pisano's Practica Geometriae (1220), and Part II, fol. 9v of Pacioli’s Summa, the scalene triangle \((13, 14, 15)\) served for fifteen centuries as the canonical benchmark of Heron’s formula because it is the smallest non-right Heronian triangle formed by gluing two primitive/scaled Pythagorean triangles—\((5, 12, 13)\) and \((9, 12, 15)\)—along their shared cathetus \(AD = 12\). Every single intermediate quantity in Pacioli’s computation (\(s = 21\), excesses \(6, 7, 8\), area \(84\), altitude \(12\), segments \(5\) and \(9\), inradius \(r = 4\), and circumradius \(R = 65/8\)) is rational, allowing the surveyor to verify Heron’s fourth-degree radical \(\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\) directly against Euclid [II.13]!

HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES

Whereas German Cossists shortly after 1489 (Johannes Widmann) adapted mercantile barrel-weight marks + and − that eventually prevailed across Northern Europe, Pacioli’s 1494 Summa standardized the Italian syncopated operators .p̄., .m̄., and .℞.u. in print for the entire sixteenth-century Italian algebraic school—adopted directly by Scipione del Ferro, Niccolò Tartaglia, Gerolamo Cardano, and Rafael Bombelli (L’Algebra, 1572).
