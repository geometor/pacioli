HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES
==========================================

.. rubric:: Commentary on the 1494 Preliminaries, Vernacular Scientific Prose & `fol. 8r`

1. Vernacular *Volgare* vs. University Latin
--------------------------------------------

Before November 1494, European mathematical print culture was sharply bifurcated: Erhard Ratdolt's 1482 Venice :cite:`*Elementa* <campanus:elements>` served Latin-reading university scholars, whereas short commercial *arte minore* pamphlets (such as the 1478 *Treviso Arithmetic* and Pietro Borghi's 1484 *Opera de Arithmetica*) gave merchants algorithmic recipes stripped of Euclidean proof. Pacioli's deliberate choice to compose a 600-page folio in Northern-Tuscan/Venetian *volgare* while retaining Latin proposition headings, marginal Euclidean citations, and Boethian terminology united the *scuola d'abbaco*, the artist-architect's *bottega*, and the university chair of mathematics into a single readership. Notice also Pacioli's inclusion of **Perspective** alongside Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, and Astronomy as a fifth mathematical discipline—anticipating his fuller argument in :ref:`*De Divina Proportione*, Compendio Cap. III <de-divina-proportione-index>` in honor of :person:`Piero della Francesca <piero-della-francesca>`.

2. Honesty of Attribution & the Debt to Leonardo Pisano (`Part I, fol. 1v`)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Modern historiography (from Baldassarre Boncompagni to Enrico Giusti) has repeatedly revisited Giorgio Vasari's polemical charge regarding Pacioli's use of :person:`Piero della Francesca's <piero-della-francesca>` manuscripts. On `Part I, fol. 1v`, Pacioli states openly that the *Summa* is a grand *compilatio* ("abbiamo cavata") from :person:`Euclid <euclid>`, :person:`Boethius <boethius>`, :person:`Leonardo Pisano <leonardo-pisano>`, :person:`Jordanus Nemorarius <jordanus-nemorarius>`, :person:`Biagio Pelacani <biagio-pelacani>`, :person:`Sacrobosco <sacrobosco>`, and :person:`Prosdocimo de' Beldomandi <prosdocimo-de-beldomandi>`, explicitly instructing the reader to credit :person:`Leonardo Pisano <leonardo-pisano>` wherever no marginal authority is named, and lavishing praise on Piero della Francesca on `fol. 68v` as *"el monarca a li tempi nostri de la pictura."*

3. Pacioli's `fol. 8r` Table of Perfect Numbers & Mersenne Composites
---------------------------------------------------------------------

On `Part I, fol. 8r` (scan `n0034`), Pacioli correctly refutes the naive medieval claim that every odd power yields a prime :math:`2^n - 1`, noting that :math:`S_n = 2^n - 1` must be tested for primality (*numero primo incomposto*) and observing Nicomachus's terminal-digit alternation (:math:`6, 8`). His printed table reaches a *Quartus decimus numerus perfectus* (:math:`2^{26}(2^{27}-1)` or higher candidates drawn from fifteenth-century Florentine *abbaco* tables such as Munich Cod. Ital. 608, investigated by Pietro Cataldi in 1603 and :person:`Pierre de Fermat <fermat>` in 1640): while the fifth perfect number :math:`33{,}550{,}336 = 2^{12}(2^{13}-1)` (`p = 13`), sixth :math:`8{,}589{,}869{,}056` (`p = 17`), and seventh :math:`137{,}438{,}691{,}328` (`p = 19`) are genuinely perfect, several higher rows in fifteenth-century tables correspond to composite Mersenne numbers (such as :math:`p = 23`, :math:`2^{23}-1 = 47 \times 178{,}481`) whose factorization exceeded hand-division prior to Fermat's congruence :math:`q \equiv 1 \pmod{2p}`.
