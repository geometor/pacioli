HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES¶

Commentary on the 1494 Preliminaries, Vernacular Scientific Prose & fol. 8r

1. Vernacular Volgare vs. University Latin¶

Before November 1494, European mathematical print culture was sharply bifurcated: Erhard Ratdolt’s 1482 Venice Elementa served Latin-reading university scholars, whereas short commercial arte minore pamphlets (such as the 1478 Treviso Arithmetic and Pietro Borghi’s 1484 Opera de Arithmetica) gave merchants algorithmic recipes stripped of Euclidean proof. Pacioli’s deliberate choice to compose a 600-page folio in Northern-Tuscan/Venetian volgare while retaining Latin proposition headings, marginal Euclidean citations, and Boethian terminology united the scuola d’abbaco, the artist-architect’s bottega, and the university chair of mathematics into a single readership. Notice also Pacioli’s inclusion of Perspective alongside Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, and Astronomy as a fifth mathematical discipline—anticipating his fuller argument in *De Divina Proportione*, Compendio Cap. III in honor of Piero della Francesca.

2. Honesty of Attribution & the Debt to Leonardo Pisano (Part I, fol. 1v)¶

Modern historiography (from Baldassarre Boncompagni to Enrico Giusti) has repeatedly revisited Giorgio Vasari’s polemical charge regarding Pacioli’s use of Piero della Francesca's manuscripts. On Part I, fol. 1v, Pacioli states openly that the Summa is a grand compilatio (“abbiamo cavata”) from Euclid, Boethius, Leonardo Pisano, Jordanus Nemorarius, Biagio Pelacani, Sacrobosco, and Prosdocimo de' Beldomandi, explicitly instructing the reader to credit Leonardo Pisano wherever no marginal authority is named, and lavishing praise on Piero della Francesca on fol. 68v as “el monarca a li tempi nostri de la pictura.”

3. Pacioli’s fol. 8r Table of Perfect Numbers & Mersenne Composites¶

On Part I, fol. 8r (scan n0034), Pacioli correctly refutes the naive medieval claim that every odd power yields a prime $2^n - 1$, noting that $S_n = 2^n - 1$ must be tested for primality (numero primo incomposto) and observing Nicomachus’s terminal-digit alternation ($6, 8$). His printed table reaches a Quartus decimus numerus perfectus ($2^{26}(2^{27}-1)$ or higher candidates drawn from fifteenth-century Florentine abbaco tables such as Munich Cod. Ital. 608, investigated by Pietro Cataldi in 1603 and Pierre de Fermat in 1640): while the fifth perfect number $33{,}550{,}336 = 2^{12}(2^{13}-1)$ (p = 13), sixth $8{,}589{,}869{,}056$ (p = 17), and seventh $137{,}438{,}691{,}328$ (p = 19) are genuinely perfect, several higher rows in fifteenth-century tables correspond to composite Mersenne numbers (such as $p = 23$, $2^{23}-1 = 47 \times 178{,}481$) whose factorization exceeded hand-division prior to Fermat’s congruence $q \equiv 1 \pmod{2p}$.
