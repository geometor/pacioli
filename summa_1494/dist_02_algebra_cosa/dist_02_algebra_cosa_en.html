ALGORISMUS, EUCLIDEAN SURDS & COSSIC ALGEBRA (REGOLA DELA COSA)¶

Part I, Distinctiones II–V (Practical Algorism) & Distinctio VIII: Arte Maggiore / Algebra e Almucabala (fols. 112r–150r)

f. Part I, fols. 19r–67v

Practical Algorism, Lattice Multiplication (Gelosia), Regola del Tre & El Cataym

In Distinctiones II–V, following Leonardo Pisano's Liber Abaci and Johannes de Sacrobosco's Algorismus, we set forth the complete practice of calculation:

  • Finger Reckoning (*Indigitatione*) & Place-Value Numeration: Hand positions signifying units through tens of thousands (fol. 36v).

  • Eight Modes of Multiplication: Scacchieri (chessboard), castelluccio, colonna, crocetta, quadrilatero, gelosia (lattice grating), repiego (by factors), and a scapezza (by parts).

  • Four Modes of Division: A regolo, per ripiego, a danda, and a galea (the galley method).

  • The Rule of Three (*Regola del Tre*) & Double False Position (*El Cataym*): Finding unknowns by two trial suppositions \((x_1, e_1)\) and \((x_2, e_2)\) before resorting to the Great Art.

f. Part I, fols. 112r–125v

Characters of the Great Art, Sign Laws & Euclid Book X Surds ([X.36], [X.73])

These are the characters with which the entire speculative practice of Algebra and Almucabala (Arte Maggiore, called by the common people the Rule of the Thing) is exercised:

  • Gradation of Powers: .n°. (Numero, \(1\)), .co. (cosa) (first unknown quantity, root or side, \(x\)), .ce. (censo) (square of the cosa, \(x^2\)), .cu. (cubo) (\(x^3\)), .ce.ce. (censo de censo) (\(x^4\)), primo relato (\(x^5\)), and ce.cu. (\(x^6\)).

  • Signs & Universal Root: .p̄. (più) and .m̄. (meno), wherein note that più by più always makes più, meno by meno always makes più, più by meno always makes meno, and meno by più always makes meno; together with .℞.u. (radice universale or legata) over binomials and apotomes (residui).

  • Six Binomials & Six Apotomes (*Residui*): Following Euclid Book X ([X.36], [X.73]), in the left figure let the binomial line AB join rational AM (\(3\)) and surd \(MB = \sqrt{5}\); its square decomposes by [II.4] into AMPQ (\(9\)), \(b = 5\), and two equal complements MBRP (\(2 \cdot 3\sqrt{5}\)), totaling \(14 + 6\sqrt{5}\). In the right figure, cutting \(NF = \sqrt{5}\) from EF (\(3\)) leaves at N the apotome (residuo) \(EN = 3 - \sqrt{5}\), whose square ENKL inside EFGH equals \(14 - 6\sqrt{5}\) ([II.7]).

f. Part I, fols. 145r–146r

Distinctio Octava, Tractatus Quintus — The Six Chapters & Geometric Gnomon Proof ([II.4]–[II.6])

To come to the knowledge of the compound chapters, following Al-Khwārizmī (Al-jabr wa'l-muqābala) and Leonardo Pisano, we first state the three simple chapters:

  1. Censo equal to cose (\(a\,x^2 = b\,x\)): divide the cose by the censi.

  2. Censo equal to numero (\(a\,x^2 = c\)): divide the numero by the censi, and the square root of the quotient is the cosa.

  3. Cose equal to numero (\(b\,x = c\)): divide the numero by the cose.

There follow the three compound chapters with their Latin verses:

  1. First Compound Canon (*Primi canonis versus*, `fol. 145r`) — Censo e cose equale a numero (\(x^2 + b x = c\)): “Si res & census numero coaequantur, a rebus dimidio sumpto censum producere debes, addere quem numero, radicem tollere toti, dimidiumque rei de radice abjicere.” Take half the cose (\(b/2\)), square it (\((b/2)^2\)), add this to the numero (\(c\)), take the square root of the whole, and subtract half the cosa from the root:

    \[x = \sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c} - \frac{b}{2}\]

  2. Second Compound Canon (*Secundi canonis versus*) — Censo e numero equale a cose (\(x^2 + c = b x\)): Multiply half the cose by itself, subtract the numero, take the root of the remainder, and either add it to or subtract it from half the cose (whenever \((b/2)^2 \ge c\), proved via [II.5]):

    \[x = \frac{b}{2} \mp \sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - c}\]

  3. Third Compound Canon (*Tertii canonis versus*) — Cose e numero equale a censo (\(b x + c = x^2\)): Take half the cose, square it, add it to the numero, take the root of the whole, and add half the cosa (proved via [II.6]):

    \[x = \sqrt{\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c} + \frac{b}{2}\]
[Euclid II.4]

Let there be for demonstration of the First Compound Chapter \(1.\text{ce.} \; \bar{p} \; 10.\text{co.} = 39\) (\(x^2 + 10x = 39\)). Set out the great square \(ABCD\) divided as shown in the margin: let the square AEFG be \(1\) censo (\(x^2\)), and let the two complements around it, EBHF and GFID, be each \(5\) cose (half of the \(10\) cose), which together with the censo make \(39\). Add the small corner square FHCI of half the cose multiplied by itself—that is, \(5 \times 5 = 25\)—to \(39\): this makes \(64\) for the whole great square \(ABCD\), whose sides AB and DA are equal to BC and CD along diagonal AC. Its square root is \(8\) for the entire side \(AB\); subtract \(EB\), which is \(5\) (half the cose), and there remains \(AE = 3\) precisely for the value of the cosa:

\[\text{Area}(ABCD) = 39 + 25 = 64 \implies AB = \sqrt{64} = 8 \implies x = AE = 8 - 5 = 3\]
f. Part I, fols. 149v–150r

Higher Gradations & Verdict on the Chapters of the Cube (fol. 150r)

Any three-term chapter whose powers stand in equidistant proportional gradation—such as censo de censo and censo equal to numero (\(a\,x^4 + b\,x^2 = c\)) or \(a\,x^6 + b\,x^3 = c\)—reduces at once to the three compound chapters above. But where numero, cosa, and cubo are compounded, or numero, censo, and cubo, or cosa, censo, and cubo equal to numero, it has not up to the present hour been possible to form general rules, on account of the disproportion of the degrees among themselves—just as no general way has yet been given to square the circle by either ancient or modern mathematicians, although in particular cases one may sometimes arrive at a solution by trial.
