HISTORICAL, PHILOLOGICAL & DEDUCTIVE NOTES
==========================================

.. rubric:: Commentary on Syncopated Typography, Book X Irrationals & the Bologna Cubic Breakthrough

1. Typographical Standardization of `.p̄.`, `.m̄.`, and `.℞.u.`
--------------------------------------------------------------

Whereas German Cossists shortly after 1489 (Johannes Widmann) adapted mercantile barrel-weight marks `+` and `−` that eventually prevailed across Northern Europe, Pacioli's 1494 *Summa* standardized the Italian syncopated operators :term:`.p̄. <piu-e-meno>`, :term:`.m̄. <piu-e-meno>`, and :term:`.℞.u. <radice-universale>` in print for the entire sixteenth-century Italian algebraic school—adopted directly by :person:`Scipione del Ferro <scipione-del-ferro>`, :person:`Niccolò Tartaglia <tartaglia>`, :person:`Gerolamo Cardano <cardano>`, and Rafael Bombelli (*L'Algebra*, 1572). Equally consequential is Pacioli's systematic bridging of :person:`Euclid's <euclid>` purely geometric Book X classification of :term:`binomials <binomio>` and :term:`apotomes (*residui*) <residuo>` with numerical surds, enabling him in both the *Summa* and :ref:`*De Divina Proportione* (Cap. VIII) <compendio-cap_08-en>` to identify the golden segments $\sqrt{125}-5$ and $15-\sqrt{125}$ rigorously as Euclidean apotomes (:just:`X.73`).

2. Why Canon 5 Admits Two Positive Roots (`fols. 145r–146v`)
------------------------------------------------------------

Because Renaissance algebra rejected negative roots (*radici ficte*), Canon 4 ($x^2 + px = q$) and Canon 6 ($px + q = x^2$) each possess a unique positive root, whereas Canon 5 ($x^2 + q = px$) represents cutting a segment of length $p$ into two positive parts $x$ and $p - x$ whose rectangle $x(p - x)$ equals $q$. By :just:`II.5`, the rectangle on unequal segments of $p$ falls short of $(p/2)^2$ by the square $(p/2 - x)^2$:

$$x(p - x) + \left(\frac{p}{2} - x\right)^2 = \left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2$$

Hence Canon 5 is soluble (*possibile*) if and only if $q \le (p/2)^2$, in which case *both* $x_1 = p/2 - \sqrt{(p/2)^2 - q}$ and $x_2 = p/2 + \sqrt{(p/2)^2 - q}$ are genuine positive segments, faithfully reflected in Pacioli's *Secundi canonis versus*: *"Quam rebus mediis addas, seu abstrahe, habebis."*

3. From `fol. 150r` (1494) to Bologna (1501–1515) & *Ars Magna* (1545)
----------------------------------------------------------------------

Pacioli's closing remark on `fol. 150r` was often misread by nineteenth-century historians as dogmatic pessimism; in fact, Pacioli carefully wrote **"finora"** (*up to the present hour*), rejecting spurious false rules circulating in fourteenth-century *abbaco* manuscripts (including Maestro Dardi of Pisa's special-case cubic recipes misapplied as universal laws by Piero della Francesca). Seven years later, during the academic year **1501–1502**, Pacioli lectured alongside :person:`Scipione del Ferro <scipione-del-ferro>` at the University of Bologna. Directly stimulated by Pacioli's printed formulation on `fol. 150r`, del Ferro succeeded around 1515 in decomposing $x = u + v$ in $x^3 + px = q$. When :person:`Gerolamo Cardano <cardano>` published the complete cubic and quartic theory in Chapter I of his :cite:`*Ars Magna* (1545) <cardano:ars-magna>`, he opened by citing Fra Luca Pacioli's exact words from `fol. 150r` as the historical watershed from which modern higher algebra sprang.
